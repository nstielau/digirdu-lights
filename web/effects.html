Choose an effect on the producer

During normal playback, press and release BOOT on the microphone FeatherS2 or the external button wired to IO43 to cycle through 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 1. If a quick tap is missed, hold for about a second, then release before pressing again. A short press takes effect on release. Hold for three seconds to sleep instead of cycling.

Each change displays its number for 1.5 seconds on the producer and connected consumers. The number uses the wing’s portrait 4×8 orientation; Spectrum uses landscape 8×4. Audio processing continues behind the number. The effect is sent in every ESP-NOW packet, so a consumer that misses a change catches up on the next accepted packet.

This page describes firmware 1.0.5, including Chroma; it does not select effects remotely. The producer starts in Spectrum after a reset unless its node configuration overrides that default. For effect selection, use BOOT without RESET.

Hold to sleep

Hold BOOT or the external IO43 button for three seconds: every pixel turns red, fades to black over three more seconds, then the board enters deep sleep. The producer repeatedly broadcasts the countdown so updated consumers fade and sleep with it. Once red appears, releasing the button does not cancel sleep.

Update all boards to 1.0.5 for shared sleep. A board that misses every sleep message remains awake. Tap RESET on each sleeping board to wake it; a producer reset cannot wake a consumer whose radio is off. Release BOOT before resetting. Sleep becomes available after the OTA health trial confirms and reboots. USB-connected boards may simulate sleep to keep the host connection.

READ THE SOUND

Spectrum

Default · blue number

Eight rainbow columns rise and fall with the sound in eight frequency bands. Start here to check that the microphone hears you and every wing is receiving updates.

What the columns mean

Frequency bands, left to right

These are frequency ranges, not instrument labels: a clap can light several columns, and a drone also produces harmonics above its fundamental. Color stays fixed across frequency, from red through green and cyan to violet. A partly lit top pixel makes changes smoother than four hard steps.

How it responds

Each band subtracts its learned background energy. All eight bands share one slowly adapting level reference, so the display preserves their relative strength instead of making every column equally tall. Quieter harmonics are lifted by amplitude compression. Bars rise quickly (50 ms smoothing) and fall more gently (300 ms smoothing); these are smoothing settings, not a guarantee of end-to-end latency.

There is no extra background glow or attack/yell overlay in Spectrum. When the room becomes quiet, the bars settle toward black. A dark wing can therefore be normal silence. Try a sustained drone, change your mouth shape, then clap: watch the low columns hold, the balance move across columns, and the clap briefly broaden the display.

HOW EFFECTS 2–4 LISTEN

One sound, several layers of light

Ember, Aurora, and Ripple share the same musical layers, with different colors and emphasis. They can respond to a drone and a vocalization at the same time. Firmware 1.0.4 expands the timbre, growl, and vocal ranges observed in the saved take so ordinary changes produce more visible movement. Strong attacks and yells also add brief whole-wing accents that fade over 0.13 and 0.32 seconds, respectively.

Drone → atmosphere
A sustained low drone supports a continuous moving field. Overall level adds a smaller contribution, and a lingering sound envelope keeps the scene alive after playing stops.
Harmonics → movement
More harmonic energy speeds the underlying wave motion and adds spatial detail. Mouth and tongue changes shift the base hue through the measured timbre position, even without a large change in loudness.
Growl → texture
The growl estimate combines midrange energy with spectral noisiness, spreading, and modulation. It adds moving, contrasting flecks to the field; volume alone does not trigger this layer.
Vocal → ribbons
Higher-frequency energy relative to the drone and total sound creates brighter moving ribbons. A strong vocal onset also launches a broader, paler yell pulse.
Attack → outward pulses
Rapid energy rises and positive spectral changes launch light fronts from the virtual player’s midpoint toward both ends. Stronger attacks make stronger pulses; their speed is theatrical, not the measured speed of sound.
Silence → afterglow
The sound envelope decays over about 3.5 seconds, pixel trails over 0.28 seconds, and pulses over 1.35 seconds. These are exponential time constants, so light fades gradually rather than shutting off on a timer.

On a single wing, physical pixels preview positions along the culvert’s length; these three effects are not frequency-bar charts. Consumers with the same coordinates mirror the scene. Per-node coordinates can give different wings different positions in the installation.

WARM & TEXTURED

Ember

Orange number

A warm red-to-orange field with a strong bed of light and pronounced growl texture.

What changes with your playing

The drone fills the wing with slow, broad waves. Timbre shifts the base color through a restrained warm range, so a mouth-shape change looks like a change in the heat of the field. Growls introduce contrasting, cooler flecks; layered vocals bring brighter ribbons across that warm base. Pops and hard articulations launch broad, pale fronts outward.

Of the three musical effects, Ember has the strongest background field and the strongest growl texture. Its base wave density is the lowest, so large areas tend to move together. Its pulses are wider than Ripple’s and narrower than Aurora’s. Trails keep the scene glowing between articulations.

Try this

Hold a steady drone, then add a low vocal growl without getting much louder. Look for texture building over the warm field. Move your tongue while sustaining the drone to explore the smaller color shifts.

3

FLOWING & LAYERED

Aurora

Mint-green number

A blue-to-violet field with denser waves, a wider timbre-driven color range, and sweeping vocal accents.

What changes with your playing

The underlying drone creates repeated folds of light. Harmonics make those folds move faster and become more closely spaced, while mouth shaping moves the base color from blue toward violet and magenta. Growl adds a gentler contrasting texture than Ember. Vocal ribbons and broad yell pulses can wash across several positions at once.

Aurora uses the highest base wave density and the widest event pulses in this library. Its timbre-to-color range is also the largest of the musical effects. Its continuous field is slightly softer than Ember’s, leaving room for layered color and broad accents.

Try this

Keep the drone’s level steady and slowly sweep your mouth from a rounded vowel to a brighter one. Watch the color and pattern change. Add a higher vocalization to bring out the ribbons and, on a strong onset, an expanding pale pulse.

4

SPACE FOR RHYTHM

Ripple

Violet number

A subdued green-to-cyan field that gives narrow, outward-moving attack pulses room to stand out.

What changes with your playing

A continuous drone still creates an environment, but the background is much dimmer than Ember or Aurora. Timbre shifts the base toward cyan. Growl texture stays restrained, while tongue attacks, pops, and hard “DOOTs” make compact light fronts travel away from the midpoint. Strong vocal onsets launch a wider front than ordinary attacks within this effect.

Ripple has the weakest field and growl texture, and the narrowest pulses, of the three musical effects. Pulse speed and decay are shared with Ember and Aurora; the quieter background and smaller pulse width make the rhythm easier to distinguish. The violet number identifies the effect, but does not dictate its green-to-cyan scene colors.

Try this

Play a sparse pattern of short attacks over a quiet drone. Leave space after each one to see the fronts travel and fade. A sustained sound without sharp changes can look deliberately subdued here.

5

FREQUENCY BECOMES COLOR

Chroma

Cyan number

One color fills the whole wing: frequency chooses the hue, volume chooses the brightness, and both blend smoothly.

153 Hz · red351 Hz · violet

How the color follows your sound

Chroma finds an approximate spectral center from the same eight bands shown by Spectrum. It weights each band’s center by its estimated relative power, after undoing the display’s amplitude compression. More high-frequency content shifts the center upward. This describes the balance of the sound; it is not a measurement of your drone’s fundamental pitch.

The color range is calibrated to the saved take: 63 active spectrum observations put the 10th and 90th percentiles at 153.28 and 350.55 Hz. A logarithmic mapping spreads that range across red, yellow, green, cyan, blue, and violet. Lower or higher values stay at the endpoint colors. Rare peaks therefore don’t squeeze ordinary playing into a tiny color range, and a loud yell won’t immediately redefine the calibration.

Brightness and blending

Only normalized volume controls brightness. Color blends with a 0.3-second time constant; brightness rises quickly over 0.06 seconds and falls more gently over 0.8 seconds. Below volume 0.10, the last hue holds while the light fades. There are no extra attack flashes or growl textures in this effect, and the 15% brightness cap remains.

Try this

Hold a drone at a steady level and change your mouth shape: watch the whole wing change color. Then keep the tone steady while playing softer and louder: watch the brightness change. Move through the saved take above to compare drone, rhythms, growls, and yells.

Listening in the culvert

Allow two quiet seconds at startup for calibration. Background estimates and level normalization adapt slowly so a single loud sound does not immediately flatten the response. All effects retain the configured 15% brightness cap.

These musical labels are tunable acoustic estimates, not guaranteed recognition of a playing technique. Echoes, microphone placement, and your instrument will affect them. Use Spectrum to understand the sound first, then compare the musical effects at a similar level.
